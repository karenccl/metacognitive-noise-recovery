# Does a static meta-d′ model track dynamic confidence? A simulation study of `hmetad` under post-decisional noise and distributional misspecification

## Abstract

**Background.** Static meta-d′ models are widely fitted to confidence ratings, although confidence may arise from dynamic, noisy post-decisional processes. **Methods.** We simulated ordinal confidence from a drift-diffusion process with post-decisional readout noise (G1), from the meta-d′ model itself (G0), and from a non-Gaussian Gumbel-min signal distribution (G2), and fitted hierarchical Bayesian meta-d′ models with `hmetad` (20 or 40 participants, 100–400 trials, 20 replications per cell; 1,440 fits). **Results.** In G1, posterior log M fell as readout noise rose in all 12 design cells (adjacent-level differences in mean log M of 0.46 to 0.57 for low−medium and 0.88 to 1.20 for medium−high); the expected ordering held in every matched replication in 11 cells and in 95% in the twelfth. G0 credible-interval coverage was 95.8% pooled across cells. In G2, leave-one-out comparison preferred the correct family in 96.2% of datasets, and Gaussian misspecification biased log M upward (+0.06 to +0.20). Across all 240 G2 datasets, the Gaussian fit's aggregate posterior predictive discrepancy was small in absolute terms (median RMSE 0.009 versus 0.003 for the correct family) but larger than the correct fit's in 96.7% of datasets; judged against the correct family's distribution of discrepancies, it was detected in 87.5% of datasets on average across cells. In a practical check that uses only the fitted model's own replicated data (24 datasets with stored full fits), a discrepancy restricted to the highest-confidence cells flagged the Gaussian fit in 20 of 24 datasets with no false alarms on the correct family; the aggregate discrepancy flagged 16 of 24, the same number as LOO's two-standard-error criterion, and 2 of 8 at 100 trials. **Conclusions.** The main finding is about misspecification: a Gaussian fit to non-Gaussian data was clearly identifiable by leave-one-out comparison and biased log M upward (coverage of the true value 81% versus 97% for the correct family). Aggregate posterior predictive discrepancy also carried the signal, but only when compared with a reference; its absolute size looked small. A targeted replicated-data check on the highest-confidence cells was a usable single-model check in this small sample, though weaker with few trials. This corrects the earlier version of this report, which read one replicate as showing that posterior predictive checks did not flag the misspecification. The ordinal recovery in G1 is best read as a validity check that a static model registers added readout noise, not as evidence that log M measures it. Conclusions are conditional on the simulated processes. **Extensions (v2.0).** Paired on the same data, the Gaussian fit's log M exceeded the correct fit's by 0.11 to 0.25, more at low d′, and the shift did not shrink with more trials while intervals narrowed. Exact refits for participants with unreliable Pareto k left every LOO conclusion unchanged. Pooling trials of two difficulty levels did not make LOO favour the Gumbel-min family. In 45 Confidence Database datasets, the Gaussian fit failed convergence diagnostics far more often than the Gumbel-min fit (76% vs 31% of datasets), so under the pre-registered rule only 2 of 17 primary perception datasets could be categorised; both favoured the Gaussian model. Five memory datasets, all from one laboratory and paradigm, favoured Gumbel-min. The empirical analysis is therefore inconclusive about which family describes real confidence data, and the asymmetric convergence failures are themselves a finding to be explained.

## 1. Background and rationale

Metacognitive efficiency is commonly estimated as the M-ratio, meta-d′/d′, from a signal detection model of confidence ratings (Maniscalco & Lau, 2012), with hierarchical Bayesian estimation available through HMeta-d (Fleming, 2017) and, in R, through `brms`-based implementations (Bürkner, 2017). These models are *static*: confidence is a fixed function of a single-trial latent variable. Dynamic accounts instead let evidence keep accumulating after a choice, so that confidence reflects post-decisional information (Pleskac & Busemeyer, 2010). If confidence is generated dynamically and read out noisily, it is not obvious that a static model's M-ratio should still order individuals or conditions sensibly.

Simulation is required because real data contain no known noise parameter, so recovery cannot be assessed on them. We therefore ask what a particular estimator does under explicitly stated generating processes (Morris et al., 2019). The study cannot show that human confidence arises from the simulated mechanism, and it does not estimate any literal neural "metacognitive noise".

## 2. Research questions and pre-specified predictions

Design decisions were written to `docs/design_decisions.md` before production runs and any deviation is dated there and in `docs/analysis_log.md`.

- **RQ1 (G1; a validity check, expected to hold).** When confidence is generated with readout noise σ_c, does posterior `log_M` from a static Gaussian model fall monotonically as σ_c rises? *Prediction:* level means ordered low > medium > high and that ordering in most replications. We do not claim that `log_M` numerically recovers σ_c; the two are on different scales.
- **RQ2 (G2; the main finding).** When the signal distribution is Gumbel-min, how do inference and predictive fit change between the correctly specified and the default Gaussian model? *Prediction:* LOO prefers the correct family, more strongly with more data.
- **RQ3.** How often do fits fail diagnostics, and where? Failure is reported as an outcome, not as bias.
- **v2.0 extensions.** (a) How much of the G2 Gaussian bias is the misspecification itself, measured against the correct fit on the same data, and does it shrink with more data? (b) Do the G2 LOO conclusions survive exact refits for participants with unreliable Pareto k? (c) Can pooling trials of different difficulty make LOO favour the Gumbel-min family when the data are Gaussian (G3)? (d) **RQ4:** On real confidence data, which family does LOO prefer, and how much does the choice change log M? The rules for (c) and (d) were committed and tagged before the corresponding fits (`v3-prereg-g3`, `v3-prereg-empirical`; `config/v3.yml`).

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Software and reproducibility
R 4.5.0, `hmetad` 0.2.1, `brms` 2.23.0, sampled with `brms`'s default `rstan` backend (rstan 2.36.0.9000, the Stan 2.36 series; confirmed from the saved fits). CmdStan 2.36.0 was installed and smoke-tested but did not run any model. All package versions are locked in `renv.lock`; see `docs/computational_environment.md`. Every simulation and fit has a deterministic seed derived from a base seed and a stable grid index (`config/grid.csv`, `config/design.yml`).

### 3.2 Data-generating processes
**Design grid.** N ∈ {20, 40} participants × T ∈ {100, 200, 400} trials × target first-order d′ ∈ {0.75, 1.5}; G1 adds three σ_c levels; 20 replications per cell. Confidence has K = 4 categories.

**G0.** Data drawn from the equal-variance Gaussian meta-d′ model with known `log_M` = −1 and no between-participant variability in d′, criterion or M (240 fits).

**G1.** Per trial, a diffusion process starts at a/2 with drift ±v, is simulated by Euler–Maruyama (dt = 1 ms, diffusion coefficient 1) until it crosses 0 or a, and then *continues* unbounded for a fixed post-decision duration τ = 0.3 s. The confidence-relevant quantity q is the post-decisional evidence relative to the starting point, sign-flipped so that positive values favour the chosen response. Readout noise is added, y = q + ε with ε ~ N(0, σ_c²), and y is converted to four confidence categories by a rank-based within-participant quartile rule. The continuous q and y are retained. Confidence is never derived from reaction time. Drift v was calibrated by bisection so that empirical d′ matched 0.75 and 1.5 (v = 0.388 and 0.797 at a = 1.5, t0 = 0.3 s); the realized pooled d′ across the production grid was 0.743 and 1.50. The three σ_c values (0.291, 0.872, 2.91) were chosen from a pilot sweep so that the confidence–accuracy association was clearly and non-arbitrarily separated (Spearman 0.287, 0.176, 0.062) (720 fits).

**G2.** Same structure as G0 but with a Gumbel-min signal distribution, fixed to unit variance and mean-centred so that its location parameter plays the role of the Gaussian mean (480 fits: each simulated dataset was fitted twice). The known `log_M` = −1 is a parameter of the Gumbel-min generator. The Gaussian fit has no generating `log_M` of its own, so its bias is the deviation of its estimate from the generating value, not the recovery of a Gaussian parameter.

### 3.3 Fitted models and priors
All fits use a hierarchical `hmetad` model with participant-level random intercepts on `log_M`, d′ and criterion, pooled confidence-threshold gaps, absolute type-2 criterion, a `normal(0, 1)` prior on population `log_M` and `normal(0, 1)` on the threshold gaps, K = 4, and an aggregated multinomial likelihood. Sampling: 4 chains × (1,000 warm-up + 1,000 draws), adapt_delta 0.95, max treedepth 12. G2 was fitted under the correct `gumbel_min` family (with matching Stan-side CDF functions) and under the default Gaussian family.

**Default priors.** Only the two priors above were set by us. `prior_summary()` of the stored fits (`outputs/tables/prior_summary.csv`; identical for G0 and both G2 fits) shows that the remaining parameters used brms defaults: the population d′ and criterion intercepts have flat (improper) priors, and all participant-level standard deviations (`log_M`, d′, criterion) have `student_t(3, 0, 2.5)` (half-t, lower-bounded at 0). The wide default on the `log_M` standard deviation is a candidate contributor to the small negative `log_M` bias (§4.7), which was not tested.

### 3.4 Diagnostics, refit and failure policy
A fit passes if max R-hat < 1.01, bulk and tail ESS ≥ 400, and there are no divergences or maximum-treedepth hits, judged on population-level effects and random-effect SDs. A flagged fit is refitted once (adapt_delta 0.99, treedepth 14). If it is still flagged it is labelled `fail`. **Pre-specified rule:** the primary analysis includes `pass` and `fail` fits (failures flagged); the sensitivity analysis uses `pass` only; `error` rows would be excluded from estimation and counted as convergence failures.

### 3.5 Metrics
G0: bias, RMSE, 95% credible-interval coverage (Wilson intervals; pooled across cells because per-cell coverage is imprecise at 20 replications). G1: per-cell means of posterior-mean `log_M` at each σ_c level, adjacent-level differences with Monte Carlo SEs, Spearman correlation, ordering accuracy (paired by replication index; σ_c levels do not share simulation seeds), and Mann–Whitney-style separation probabilities. G2: paired ELPD difference (correct − misspecified) from LOO, whose observation unit is one *participant's* aggregated count vector, and `log_M` bias/RMSE for each family. Robustness: prior width, K = 6 re-binning of the G1 data (G0 cannot be re-binned; see Limitations), and posterior predictive checks.

**Posterior predictive analysis of all G2 fits (added in v1.1).** Specified in `docs/design_decisions.md` ("v2 extension 1") before any statistic was computed, after the v1.0 results had been seen. Using the posterior-mean predicted cell probabilities saved for every G2 fit, three per-fit discrepancies were computed: (1) the aggregate RMSE of observed − predicted cell proportions, counts pooled over participants (the v1.0 statistic; primary); (2) the mean absolute discrepancy over the four highest-confidence cells (secondary, motivated by the v1.0 replicate); (3) a participant-level Pearson X² divided by its degrees of freedom, scaled by sampling noise and used only as a discrepancy measure, never referred to a χ² distribution. Each statistic was compared within dataset (Gaussian − correct) and by *oracle-calibrated detection*: in each N × T × d′ cell, a Gaussian fit counts as detected if its statistic exceeds the 19th of the 20 correct-family values (a 5% false-alarm rate among correct fits). Pooled detection is the mean of the 12 per-cell rates. Because an analyst would not know the correct family's reference distribution, detection rates describe what the discrepancy can reveal given a reference, not what an unaided analyst would notice. A decision rule for the v1.0 claim (kept, qualified, reversed or mixed) was fixed in advance.

**Replicated-data posterior predictive check (added in v1.2).** Specified in `docs/design_decisions.md` ("v2 extension 2") before any statistic was computed, after the v1.1 results had been seen. It uses the 48 G2 fits whose full posterior was stored (replications 1–2 in each of the 12 cells, both families; 24 datasets). For each of the 4,000 posterior draws, replicated counts were drawn from the multinomial for each participant × stimulus with that draw's predicted cell probabilities and the observed trial counts, matching the aggregated likelihood used in fitting. The three discrepancies above were computed as realised discrepancies against each draw's own probabilities, for both the observed and the replicated data, and the posterior predictive p-value is the proportion of draws in which the replicated discrepancy was at least as large as the observed one. A fit was flagged if p < 0.05. This needs nothing beyond the fitted model itself. Before any statistic, the script checked that per-draw probabilities summed to 1 and that their draw means reproduced the stored predictions. A decision rule was fixed in advance (not interpretable if more than 2 of 24 correct fits were flagged by the aggregate RMSE; detects at ≥ 18/24 Gaussian fits flagged; misses at ≤ 6/24; partial otherwise).

### 3.6 v2.0 extensions
**Relative bias.** For each of the 240 G2 datasets, δ_mis = posterior-mean log M(Gaussian) − log M(correct) on the same data, summarised per cell with Monte Carlo SEs. The low- vs high-d′ contrast treats cells as independent samples (cells do not share seeds), and a linear model of δ_mis on log₂T, N and d′ tests whether the shift persists with data. No refitting.

**Exact-refit LOO.** For the 48 stored G2 fits, every participant with Pareto k > 0.7 was refitted without that participant (`brms` reloo; the left-out participant is predicted as a new level) and the PSIS and exact comparisons were contrasted pair by pair. A pre-specified rule kept the PSIS conclusion if the > 2 SE classification agreed in ≥ 90% of pairs.

**G3: difficulty mixture.** Gaussian meta-d′ data (N = 40, 200 trials per level, true log M = −1) in two conditions: control (both halves d′ = 1.25) and mixture (d′ 0.5 and 2.0, same mean), 20 replications, each fitted with Gaussian and Gumbel-min families either pooled over difficulty or with difficulty as a covariate on d′ and criterion (160 fits). Pre-specified verdict: a "pooling artefact" if the pooled comparison favoured Gumbel-min by > 2 SE in ≥ 25% of mixture replications and ≤ 10% of control replications.

**Empirical analysis (RQ4).** Data: the Confidence Database (Rahnev et al., 2020; OSF `s46pr`, downloaded 2026-09-30, CC0, md5-verified). Automatic screening (two stimuli and responses, a discrete confidence scale of 3–11 levels, ≥ 20 participants with ≥ 100 trials) left 66 of 180 datasets; the 53 in perception or memory were coded by hand for how difficulty was manipulated (constant, recorded levels, staircase, unrecorded variation, unclear) from their readmes, before any real-data fit. Sets: primary (perception, constant or ≤ 4 recorded levels), sensitivity (staircase), sensitivity_unrecorded (unrecorded variation, admitted because G3 found no pooling artefact) and secondary (memory). Participants with accuracy outside 0.55–0.95 were excluded; sparse confidence levels (< 5% of trials) were merged; datasets with more than 100 remaining participants kept a seeded random 100. 45 datasets were usable (17 primary, 7, 4, 17). Each was fitted with both families: hierarchical d′, criterion, log M and confidence thresholds, recorded condition as a covariate on d′, criterion and log M. Because participants differ in thresholds, the empirical models have participant-level thresholds (the simulation models pooled them). LOO was repaired by grouped 5-fold cross-validation by participant (shared folds), because nearly every participant had k > 0.7. **Categories (pre-specified):** clear Gaussian or clear Gumbel-min if |elpd difference| > 2 SE, otherwise inconclusive; *unreliable* if either family's fit failed diagnostics or the repair failed.

**Deviations from the pre-registration (all dated in `docs/design_decisions.md`).** Before the main run, after trial fits: narrower random initial values (`init_r` 0.5), sampling started at adapt_delta 0.99 with escalation to 0.999, and the 100-participant cap. After 16 of the first 18 fits failed diagnostics: a `normal(0, 0.5)` prior on every participant-level SD (instead of the `student_t(3, 0, 2.5)` default) and 2,000 + 2,000 draws. This decision was made on convergence diagnostics only; the 34 fits under the registered configuration are kept for comparison. The configuration was validated by refitting 30 G0/G2 datasets (recovery comparable to v1) and on Gaussian data with between-participant spread (true SD of log M 0.5; 5 replications per prior). Before any LOO was read, three additions were fixed: a flag when the posterior 97.5% quantile of the log M SD exceeds 1.0; an exploratory table of diagnostic failures by family; and a sensitivity categorisation that also admits fits failing *only* on divergences. LOO repair was run only for the 22 datasets whose category could depend on it (both fits passing, or failing only on divergences); the other 23 are unreliable under both rules whatever their LOO.

### 3.7 Spurious group differences (v3b)
§4.8 showed that the Gaussian shift in log M is larger at low d′. If two groups differ only in d′, a misspecified model could then report a group difference in log M that does not exist. Two checks, with rules committed and tagged (`v3b-prereg`, `config/v3b.yml`) before any analysis; the d′ contrast in Δ_mis was already known. **(B) Separate fits:** each G2 cell at d′ = 0.75 was paired with the cell at d′ = 1.5 with the same N, T and replication (independent datasets, true log M equal), and the posterior of log M(high d′) − log M(low d′) was formed by pairing draws at random; a false positive is a 95% interval excluding 0 (120 pairs per family, no refitting). **(C) One model:** 20 replications of two groups of 20 participants (d′ 0.75 and 1.5, T = 200, true log M −1 in both) simulated from the Gumbel-min generator and fitted once with each family, with a group effect on d′, criterion and log M (v1 production settings); a false positive is a 95% interval for the log M group effect excluding 0. **Framing rule (pre-specified):** the bias counts as a practically relevant spurious-difference problem if the Gaussian false-positive rate is ≥ 0.20 in any N × T cell of B, or exceeds the correct model's by ≥ 0.10 in B or in C; otherwise it is reported as a shift common to both groups that mainly affects absolute log M.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 G0: recovery when the model is correct (Fig. 2)
Posterior mean `log_M` was negatively biased in all 12 cells (−0.177 to −0.025), and RMSE generally fell with more data and higher d′ (from 0.326 at N = 20, T = 200, d′ = 0.75 to 0.069 at N = 40, T = 400, d′ = 1.5), though not strictly monotonically in T within d′ = 0.75. Pooled coverage was 95.8% (230/240; 95% CI 92.5–97.7%; MCSE 1.3%). Per-cell coverage ranged from 80% to 100%, which is uninformative on its own at 20 replications per cell.

### 4.2 G1: ordinal recovery (Fig. 3)
All 720 G1 fits passed. In all 12 N × T × d′ cells the level means were ordered low > medium > high. Mean `log_M` ranged from −0.30 to −0.18 (low), −0.84 to −0.66 (medium) and −1.93 to −1.71 (high); adjacent differences were 0.46–0.57 (low−medium) and 0.88–1.20 (medium−high), the smallest being 7.2 Monte Carlo SEs. Spearman ρ was between −0.92 and −0.94, and separation probabilities for adjacent noise levels were ≥ 0.965. Ordering accuracy was 100% (20/20 matched replications) in 11 cells and 95% (19/20) in the remaining one; results with the posterior median were the same (0.95–1.00). (The earlier linear-slope summary and its p-values were dropped: the three levels are not equally spaced and p-values on simulation replicates only reflect the number of replications.) Pass-only sensitivity results are identical because there were no failures. The K = 6 re-binning check (G1 only; 5 replications per level, 15 fits, identical continuous evidence and responses) gave the same ordering accuracy (100% at K = 4 and K = 6); posterior medians moved by 0.027 on average (max 0.099) and all 15 paired intervals overlapped.

### 4.3 G2: distributional misspecification (Fig. 4)
The correct family was preferred in 96.2% of the 240 paired datasets (72.9% by more than two standard errors). One LOO observation is one participant's aggregated response–confidence count vector, not a single trial, so ELPD differences describe participant-level predictive fit. The mean ELPD advantage grew from +2.2 at N = 20, T = 100, d′ = 0.75 to +83.1 at N = 40, T = 400, d′ = 1.5; in the least informative cells the preference was weak (as few as 10% of pairs exceeded two SE). The correctly specified fit had a small negative `log_M` bias in all 12 cells (−0.092 to −0.007; RMSE 0.042–0.272). The Gaussian fit was biased *upward* in all 12 cells (+0.064 to +0.195; RMSE 0.105–0.299), i.e. it made participants look more metacognitively efficient than the generating truth. **Pareto-k caveat:** 12.0% (correct) and 8.2% (misspecified) of LOO observations had k > 0.7, affecting most fits (181/240 and 165/240 had at least one such participant). No remedy was applied in v1, so ELPD magnitudes are approximate; the direction is supported by its consistency across pairs. In v2.0, exact refits on the 24 stored pairs left every conclusion unchanged (§4.9).

### 4.4 Model adequacy (Figs. 5–7)
*Representative fits (Fig. 5).* For five representative fits (N = 40, T = 200, d′ = 1.5, replication 1: G0, G1 low noise, G1 high noise, G2 correct, G2 misspecified) the RMSE of observed minus predicted cell proportions was 0.0024–0.0072, small for all. The misspecified fit had the largest discrepancy (0.0072 versus 0.0038 for the correct fit on the same data), and its four largest cell errors were all at the highest confidence category. G1 had no failed fit to display.

*All G2 fits (Fig. 6, `outputs/figures/v2/fig_g2_ppc_all.png`; added in v1.1).* All 480 G2 fits had saved predictions (478 pass, 2 fail; 240 pairs). The v1.0 statistic was reproduced exactly for the representative pair. The Gaussian fit had the larger aggregate RMSE in 96.7% of datasets. The median RMSE was 0.0086 for the Gaussian fit and 0.0031 for the correct one, a median within-dataset ratio of 2.64 (90% range 1.09–7.88). The highest-confidence discrepancy separated the fits more sharply (median ratio 6.82; Gaussian worse in 95.8% of datasets). The participant-level X² separated them least (median ratio 1.09; worse in 94.6%).

Oracle-calibrated detection of the Gaussian fit, averaged over the 12 cells, was 0.875 for the aggregate RMSE, 0.933 for the highest-confidence discrepancy and 0.487 for the participant-level X². Detection by the aggregate RMSE rose with T, N and d′. It was 1.00 in 8 of 12 cells and lowest at N = 20, T = 100, d′ = 0.75 (0.15; Wilson 95% CI 0.05–0.36). In every cell it was at least as high as the proportion of datasets in which LOO preferred the correct family by more than two standard errors (0.10 in that cell). This comparison is not like-for-like: the PPC threshold uses the correct family's own distribution (an oracle), whereas LOO's > 2 SE criterion is a conservative decision available from a single dataset. It shows that the discrepancy is informative given a reference, not that posterior predictive checks are more sensitive than LOO. The participant-level X² reached 0.80 only at N = 40, T = 400, d′ = 1.5. Results were the same when only pairs in which both fits passed diagnostics were used (0.874 for the aggregate RMSE).

Under the pre-specified decision rule the v1.0 claim, that aggregate posterior predictive discrepancy did not reveal the misspecification, is **reversed**. The v1.0 replicate was not unusual (its ratio of 1.9 lies within the range above). What was wrong was the reading: a discrepancy that is small in absolute terms was taken to mean that it did not reveal the misspecification. The three predictions written before the analysis were not supported. The median RMSE ratio exceeded the predicted bound of 1.5. The highest-confidence discrepancy had a larger ratio but not a higher proportion of pairs with the Gaussian fit worse. Detection was not below LOO's rate in any cell.

*Practical check with replicated data (Fig. 7, `outputs/figures/v2/fig_g2_ppp.png`; added in v1.2).* All 48 stored fits passed diagnostics and the preconditions (maximum deviations below 1e-15). With p < 0.05 as the flag, the highest-confidence discrepancy flagged 20 of 24 Gaussian fits (Wilson 95% CI 0.64–0.93) and 0 of 24 correct fits (0–0.14). The aggregate RMSE flagged 16 of 24 Gaussian fits (0.47–0.82) and 0 correct fits. On the same 24 datasets, LOO preferred the correct family by more than two standard errors in 16, and the oracle threshold of extension 1 detected 22. The aggregate RMSE flagged 2 of 8 Gaussian fits at T = 100 and 7 of 8 at both T = 200 and T = 400 (6 of 12 at N = 20, 10 of 12 at N = 40); the highest-confidence discrepancy flagged 4 of 8 at T = 100. The participant-level X² flagged 15 of 24 Gaussian fits but also 9 of 24 correct fits (6 of 8 at T = 400), so it does not separate the families and is not a usable check here. Median p-values for the aggregate RMSE were 0.52 (correct) and 0.010 (Gaussian).

Under the pre-specified rule the result is **partial**: 16 of 24 is between the "misses" (≤ 6) and "detects" (≥ 18) thresholds, with no false alarms. The rule also names the highest-confidence discrepancy, which met its condition (≥ 18 flagged, ≤ 2 false alarms), as a targeted check that did flag the misspecification. Of the predictions, correct fits were rarely flagged by the aggregate and highest-confidence statistics but not by the X²; practical detection was below the oracle, as predicted; and detection was not concentrated at T = 400 but appeared from T = 200. With two datasets per cell, per-cell results are not interpretable and every rate has a wide interval.

### 4.5 Convergence and failure (RQ3)
Across 1,440 fits: 1,437 pass, 3 fail (G0 N = 40/T = 400/d′ = 0.75; two G2-correct fits at T = 100, d′ = 0.75, both divergence-driven), 0 errors; no fit reached maximum treedepth. Escalation was more patterned than failure: 7.5–12.5% by generator, up to 55% in low-d′, short-T, high-σ_c cells. Diagnostics describe each row's final attempt only.

### 4.6 Sensitivity to the prior (single replicate)
Refitting one G0 and one G1 dataset under `normal(0, 0.5)`, `normal(0, 1)` and `normal(0, 2)` shifted the posterior mean monotonically toward zero as the prior tightened (G0: −0.939, −0.958, −0.966; G1: −0.722, −0.733, −0.736), a modest range relative to posterior SDs of about 0.07–0.09. This argues against shrinkage from the `log_M` prior toward zero as the *cause* of the negative bias in 4.1 and 4.3 (the priors on the participant-level SDs were not varied), because shrinkage toward zero would push a true −1 toward less negative values; but one replicate per generator cannot resolve a bias of this size, and the source of the small negative bias is not established here.

### 4.7 Post-hoc diagnostics of bias and coverage (exploratory)
These analyses were added after the main results (2026-09-28) and use only the saved posterior draws (`code/03_recovery_analysis/bias_diagnostics.R`); they are not part of the pre-specified plan.

*Bias.* Pooled over fits, the `log_M` bias of the posterior mean was −0.075 (G0) and −0.044 (G2-correct), and −0.056 and −0.035 for the posterior median; it was negative in about two thirds of fits (66%) and shrank with more data (G0: −0.10 at N = 20, T = 200 vs −0.05 at N = 40, T = 400). The signed error was negatively correlated with each fit's posterior between-participant SD of `log_M` (Pearson −0.48 in G0 and −0.33 in G2-correct; median within-cell correlation −0.54 and −0.33), although the true SD is 0. So fits that infer more spurious between-participant spread also sit lower. This is compatible with a prior/hierarchy effect but does not establish it, and we did not test alternative priors on the SD.

*G2 coverage of the true `log_M` = −1.* The correct-family fit covered 97.1% (233/240; Wilson 94.1–98.6%), consistent with nominal coverage or mild over-coverage. The Gaussian fit covered 81.2% (195/240; 75.8–85.7%), and coverage fell with more data (45% at N = 40, T = 400, d′ = 0.75), as expected for a biased estimator with shrinking intervals.

*Other parameters.* In the correct fit, d′ and criterion intercepts were recovered essentially without bias (d′ bias 0.001, RMSE 0.039; c bias −0.002, RMSE 0.020). The Gaussian fit over-estimated d′ (+0.073; 51% interval coverage) and under-estimated the criterion (−0.113; 7% coverage). These compare fitted values to the nominal generating values; for the Gaussian fit on Gumbel data they measure compensation for the wrong link rather than estimation error, since the two latent scales are not the same. The metacognitive gap intercepts (assumed truth −1) were 87–88% covered even for the correct model and G0, so that comparison is treated as descriptive only.

### 4.8 The misspecification shift relative to the correct fit (Fig. 8)
On the same simulated data, the Gaussian fit's log M exceeded the correct fit's by 0.19 to 0.25 in the six d′ = 0.75 cells and by 0.11 to 0.15 in the six d′ = 1.5 cells. The shift was larger at low d′ in 6/6 N × T cells (pooled difference 0.097, SE 0.011). It did not shrink with more trials: the slope on log₂T was +0.0003 (95% CI −0.013 to +0.014) with an intercept of 0.22, and N had no detectable effect. Meanwhile the Gaussian interval narrowed (mean width 0.99 at T = 100 to 0.49 at T = 400, d′ = 0.75), so the share of Gaussian intervals excluding the true value rose from 0.13 to 0.35 (d′ = 0.75) and from 0.05 to 0.33 (d′ = 1.5): with more data the misspecified model becomes more confidently wrong. The correct fit's own bias (−0.007 to −0.092) did not differ from the G0 bias in the same cell by more than 2 SE in any of the 12 cells, so the small negative baseline belongs to the fitting procedure, not to the Gumbel generator, and δ_mis isolates the misspecification.

### 4.9 Exact-refit LOO (Fig. 9)
Thirty of the 48 stored fits had at least one participant with k > 0.7; 142 leave-one-participant-out refits all succeeded. The correct family was preferred in 23/24 pairs under both PSIS and exact LOO and by > 2 SE in 16/24 under both; sign and 2-SE agreement were 24/24, the median absolute change in the ELPD difference was 0.03 and the largest 0.87. By the pre-specified rule, the PSIS conclusion stands. The 216 pairs without stored fits could not be refitted; their Pareto-k profile by cell is in `data/processed/v3/g2_pareto_k_by_cell.csv`.

### 4.10 G3: pooling over difficulty (Fig. 10)
All 160 fits passed after escalation (one initial run failed on a transient socket error and was rerun with the same seed). Pooled over difficulty, LOO favoured Gumbel-min by > 2 SE in 0/20 mixture and 0/20 control replications; the true Gaussian model was favoured by > 2 SE in 20/20 replications in every condition and aggregation (median ELPD advantage 28.7 for mixture pooled, 30.8 for control pooled, 57.1 for mixture with difficulty modelled). **Verdict (pre-specified): no pooling artefact.** The Gaussian fits were essentially unbiased (−0.014 to −0.017; coverage 0.85–1.00), whereas the Gumbel-min fits on these Gaussian data sat 0.46–0.50 below the truth (coverage 0). The plan had expected the two families' log M to agree on Gaussian data; they differed by +0.45 to +0.48. This is the mirror image of §4.8 (there the Gaussian fit to Gumbel data was shifted up), and it means that a large between-family log M difference on real data says nothing by itself about which family is right.

### 4.11 Empirical comparison on the Confidence Database (RQ4; Fig. 11)
*Convergence.* All 90 fits completed without error. The Gaussian fit failed diagnostics in 34 of 45 datasets and the Gumbel-min fit in 14 (divergences 67% vs 18% of fits; R-hat 49% vs 24%; bulk ESS 33% vs 13%; tail ESS 27% vs 4%). Paired by dataset, only the Gaussian fit failed in 21 datasets, only the Gumbel-min fit in 1, both in 13 and neither in 10 (exploratory). The misspecified Gaussian model converged in 10/10 simulation fits under the same configuration (§3.6 validation), so these failures are not simply a consequence of misspecification; they point to a feature of real data that the Gaussian model handles badly, which was not identified here.

The two categorised primary datasets, Gherman_2018 (Gherman & Philiastides, 2018) and OHora_unpub_1 (unpublished; contributed to the database by O'Hora and colleagues), favoured the Gaussian family (ELPD difference +132, SE 50, and +133, SE 40). The five Gumbel-favouring memory datasets (Kantner & Lindsay, 2012, Exps. 1 and 3; Kantner & Lindsay, 2014, Exps. 1 and 2; Lindsay et al., 2014; −82 to −251) all come from one laboratory and one paradigm, old/new recognition with three confidence levels derived from a six-point scale, so they are not independent evidence. Every analysed dataset, with its source, is listed in Supplement S15.

*Divergence-only sensitivity (pre-specified, exploratory).* Admitting fits whose only failure was divergences: primary 3 clear Gaussian, 2 inconclusive, 12 unreliable; memory 6 clear Gaussian, 5 clear Gumbel-min, 2 inconclusive, 4 unreliable; staircase and unrecorded sets 1 clear Gaussian and 1 inconclusive each. No dataset changed direction between the two rules, and every additionally admitted dataset was clear Gaussian or inconclusive. Because the excluded datasets are mostly those where the Gaussian fit failed, neither rule gives an unbiased tally of which family real data prefer.

*log M.* The Gaussian log M exceeded the Gumbel-min log M in every perception dataset (median difference 0.47 primary, 0.38 staircase, 0.43 unrecorded) and in most memory datasets (median 0.14). In the five Gumbel-favouring datasets the difference was small and negative (−0.04 to −0.18). Given §4.10, these differences describe how much the reported log M depends on the assumed family, not which family is correct. In multi-condition datasets, 5 of 27 condition contrasts in log M changed whether their 95% interval excluded zero depending on the family. Posterior predictive p-values flagged both families often (aggregate RMSE: Gaussian 27/45, Gumbel-min 38/44; one Gumbel-min fit not computed because its predicted probabilities failed a numerical check).

*Prior check.* Five datasets were flagged (posterior 97.5% quantile of the log M SD > 1.0); only one is categorised (OHora_unpub_1, flagged by its Gumbel-min fit at 1.008).

### 4.12 Spurious group differences (v3b; Fig. 12)
*Separate fits (B).* The estimated difference log M(high d′) − log M(low d′) averaged −0.065 (MCSE 0.016) for the Gaussian fits and +0.032 (MCSE 0.014) for the correct fits, in line with §4.8. Its 95% interval excluded zero in 4 of 120 Gaussian pairs (0.033; Wilson 0.013–0.083) and 3 of 120 correct pairs (0.025; 0.009–0.071). The Gaussian rate was 0 in four of six N × T cells, 0.05 at N = 40, T = 200 and 0.15 (3/20) at N = 40, T = 400; the correct model's rate was 0.05 and 0.10 in the same two cells.

*One model (C).* All 40 fits passed diagnostics. The log M group effect averaged −0.119 (MCSE 0.046) for the Gaussian fits and −0.047 (0.046) for the correct fits; its interval excluded zero in 2 of 20 Gaussian fits (0.10; 0.03–0.30) and 1 of 20 correct fits (0.05; 0.01–0.24).

*Verdict (pre-specified rule): not met* (largest Gaussian cell rate in B 0.15 < 0.20; excess over the correct model 0.008 in B and 0.05 in C, both < 0.10). Under the pre-specified wording, the misspecification bias acts mainly as a shift common to both groups, affecting absolute log M values (for example, comparisons with M = 1 or across studies; §4.8) more than comparisons between groups that differ in d′. This means only that there was no evidence of a practically relevant rise in false positives at these sample sizes, not that group comparisons are robust: the rates are imprecise (with 20 replications, C cannot distinguish a rate of 0.05 from one of about 0.25). The shift is also not entirely common. On the same simulated data, the Gaussian group difference was more negative than the correct model's by 0.097 (MCSE 0.011) in B and 0.072 (0.015) in C (`data/processed/v3b/*_differential_bias.csv`; descriptive, computed after the results). This d′-dependent part of the bias is clearly non-zero, but at N = 20–40 per group and T ≤ 400 it is small relative to the posterior uncertainty of a group difference, so it rarely moved an interval away from zero; the largest false-positive rate occurred in the largest cell. Because the bias does not shrink with data while intervals do (§4.8), spurious differences may become more frequent with larger groups or more trials; this was not tested beyond the grid. The Gaussian group effect in C (−0.119) was about twice the mean difference in B (−0.065). C differs in design (one cell, N = 20, T = 200; one model in which both groups share the confidence thresholds, whereas separate fits estimate them per group), but the misspecified − correct difference was similar in the two checks, and the gap between −0.119 and −0.065 (≈ 0.05, SE ≈ 0.05) is within Monte Carlo error, so it is not interpreted.

## 5. Discussion

**Framing.** RQ1 is a validity check: readout noise removes information from confidence, so a working confidence model should register it, and the result would have been alarming had it failed. The informative results are those for RQ2, where the static model was fitted to data that violate its distributional assumption. The failure was visible to LOO and in the parameter estimates. It was also present in aggregate posterior predictive discrepancy, but only as a systematic difference relative to the correct model, not as a discrepancy that looks large in absolute terms.

**What the results support.** Under this generator, a static Gaussian meta-d′ model fitted hierarchically ordered conditions by readout noise reliably, across the full range of sample sizes tried. This supports using `log_M` as an *ordinal* index of confidence quality in designs like these. It does not show that `log_M` measures σ_c: only ordering was tested, and the two are on different scales.

**Parameter trend versus model adequacy.** In G2, a small-looking predictive discrepancy coexisted with genuine misspecification. LOO separated the models clearly and increasingly with data. The Gaussian fit's aggregate discrepancy was only about 0.009 in proportion units, yet it was typically between two and three times the correct fit's (median ratio 2.64) and, given the correct fit's distribution as a reference, detected the misspecification in every cell at least as often as LOO preferred the correct family by more than two standard errors (an oracle comparison that does not imply greater sensitivity than LOO; §4.4). Discrepancy concentrated in the highest-confidence cells. Point estimates were also systematically distorted (upward bias under the Gaussian fit). So a reasonable-looking `log_M` trend and a small absolute PPC error should not be read as evidence that the fitted static model is a generatively adequate account of the confidence distribution. The information is in the comparison, not the absolute magnitude.

**Implications.** Analysts who fit static meta-d′ models to data plausibly generated by noisy post-decisional accumulation can expect the *ordering* of efficiency across conditions to be preserved. Before interpreting the absolute level of M, they should check distributional fit comparatively: compare alternative signal distributions with LOO, or compare posterior predictive discrepancies across candidate models, paying particular attention to the highest-confidence cells. Judging a single model's discrepancy by its absolute size was not sensitive here. When only one model is fitted, a replicated-data posterior predictive check targeted at the highest-confidence cells is a reasonable check: in 24 datasets it flagged the Gaussian fit in 20 without false alarms. The aggregate discrepancy was less sensitive, and both were weaker with 100 trials per participant. These rates come from a small sample and one form of misspecification, so they indicate that such a check can work, not how often it will.

**The v2.0 extensions.** Measured against the correct model on the same data, the Gaussian shift in log M is a displacement, not a small-sample bias: it does not shrink with trials, and because intervals narrow, more data makes the misspecified estimate more confidently wrong (§4.8). The LOO conclusions of v1 do not depend on the unreliable Pareto-k values (§4.9), and pooling difficulty levels did not create a spurious preference for the Gumbel-min family (§4.10). On real data the question of which family fits better could not be settled (§4.11). Most datasets failed the pre-registered diagnostic rule, and the failures were concentrated in the Gaussian fits, so the datasets that could be categorised are a selected subset. Where categories could be assigned, perception data favoured the Gaussian family and one laboratory's recognition-memory data favoured Gumbel-min. Two conclusions are safe. First, the family assumption changes the reported log M substantially (by about 0.4 on the log scale in perception data), and simulation shows that such a difference arises whichever family is wrong; analysts should therefore report it, not treat it as noise. Second, the Gaussian model's convergence failures on real data, absent in simulation, are a practical warning sign worth investigating in their own right. Finally, the Gaussian shift depends on d′ and biases the difference between groups that differ only in d′ (by about 0.07–0.10 on the log scale relative to the correct model), but at the sample sizes simulated this rarely produced a spurious group difference (§4.12). The false-positive rates are imprecise, and larger studies may fare worse. Its main consequence is for the absolute level of log M.

**Limitations.** (i) The RQ1 test is comparatively favourable: adding independent noise to the readout of one evidence stream removes information in a way any monotone confidence model should register; other dynamic mechanisms (e.g. confidence depending on decision time, or separate evidence streams) were not simulated. (ii) σ_c levels were calibrated at d′ = 1.5 and reused at d′ = 0.75. (iii) `log_M` truth was fixed at −1 in G0/G2, one confidence-threshold structure was used, and G0/G2 have no between-participant variability in d′, criterion or M (thresholds vary slightly between simulated participants but were pooled in the fitted model). (iv) A small negative `log_M` bias (12/12 cells in both G0 and G2-correct) is only partly characterised: it shrinks with data and correlates with the posterior between-participant SD (§4.7), but its cause was not tested. (v) LOO has unreliable Pareto-k values for a substantial share of observations; in v2.0 exact refits on the 24 stored pairs confirmed every conclusion, but the other 216 pairs were not refitted. (vi) The K = 6 (n = 5 per level) and prior (n = 1 per generator) checks are small. The PPC analysis covers all 240 G2 datasets (§4.4) but not G0 or G1 beyond one replicate each, uses posterior-mean predictions rather than replicated datasets (predictive draws were not saved), and calibrates detection against the correct family's own distribution, which a real analyst would not have. The replicated-data check (v1.2) that removes the oracle covers only the 24 datasets with stored full fits (two per cell, selected by the storage rule, not by outcome), and posterior predictive p-values are conservative. Its participant-level X² flagged 9 of 24 correct fits; one untested explanation is that simulated participants' confidence thresholds vary slightly while the fitted models pool them (see iii). The v1.0 conclusion on posterior predictive checks, drawn from one replicate, was wrong and has been reversed. The K = 6 check is a valid re-binning only for G1; G0 draws discrete confidence directly from hmetad's generative model, so a K = 6 G0 dataset is a different dataset (same-seed responses agree on 95.1% of trials in the checked cell) and the G0 K = 4/K = 6 differences (mean 0.21, max 0.38) confound binning with data and are not evidence about bin-count sensitivity. Sensitivity of G0 to bin count is therefore untested. (vii) Per-replicate tie/cut-point diagnostics of the discretisation were not persisted in the compact output format. (viii) σ_c levels do not share seeds, so ordering accuracy is an independent-replicate comparison. (ix) No cross-check against JAGS HMeta-d and no open-data illustration were done. (x) Cross-distribution `log_M` comparisons are within `hmetad`'s parameterisation and do not claim identical substantive scale. (xi) Priors on d′, criterion (flat) and participant-level SDs (`student_t(3, 0, 2.5)`) are brms defaults that were not varied in the simulation grid; the empirical analysis used `normal(0, 0.5)` on the SDs, checked only on 30 G0/G2 refits and on one heterogeneous setting (true SD 0.5, five replications). (xii) Single simulation seed base, one hardware/software environment, and rstan only; no cross-backend or cross-seed replication of the whole grid. (xiii) Empirical analysis (v2.0): the primary categorisation rests on 2 perception datasets (5 under the divergence-only rule) and is a selected subset because exclusions fell mostly on the Gaussian fits; the memory result rests on one laboratory; the SD prior and longer chains were adopted after the registration, on convergence grounds; datasets with more than 100 participants were subsampled; refit convergence in the K-fold repair was verified on one pilot dataset only; and the repair was not run for the 23 datasets that are unreliable under both rules.

**Next steps.** Find out why the Gaussian model fails to converge on real data but not in simulation (for example, sparse high-confidence cells or between-participant spread larger than simulated); test the SD prior at larger true SDs; test whether the participant-level X² false alarms come from pooled confidence thresholds; extend the replicated-data check beyond the 24 stored fits; vary the participant-level SD prior to test the bias explanation in §4.7; test alternative dynamic confidence mechanisms and signal distributions beyond Gumbel-min; cross-check with HMeta-d.

## 6. Reproducibility statement

Code, configuration, seeds, compact per-fit results (`data/processed/`), figures and a dated decision/analysis log are in this repository. Full posterior objects are not stored except for replications 1–2 per cell (`data/interim/`, git-ignored). Run order: `make all` (setup, grid, G1, G0, G2, compile, analyse, figures), plus `make robustness` for the two refit-based checks (prior sweep and K = 6; roughly 20 and 75 minutes). The full grid took about 26 hours on one 22-core machine (see README). Deviations from the locked design are listed in `docs/design_decisions.md`: `qs2` replaced the archived `qs`; explicit aggregation before fitting; replications reduced from 30 to 20 after measured throughput (~55 fits/hour); compact result records instead of full fit objects; chain-parallel sampling. Errors found and corrected during the project, all recorded in `docs/analysis_log.md`: K-generic simulation; a hardcoded interpretation string; a misattributed bias range; the fitting backend (rstan, not CmdStan); the claim that the G0 K = 6 check was a re-binning; and, in v1.1, the conclusion that aggregate posterior predictive discrepancy did not reveal the G2 misspecification (§4.4). The v1.1 analysis is `code/04_ppc_and_comparison/g2_ppc_all.R`; the v1.2 replicated-data check is `code/04_ppc_and_comparison/g2_ppp_fullfits.R` (about 15 minutes; needs the stored full fits in `data/interim/`). The v2.0 analyses are listed in the README (v3 section) and `docs/v3_plan.md`; `Rscript code/run_v3.R` reruns every step that needs no refitting, and the Makefile `v3-*` targets regenerate everything (about 6.5 hours for G3, 55 hours for the empirical fits and two days for the LOO repair on the same machine). The Confidence Database is not redistributed; `docs/data_access_confidence_db.md` gives the source and checksums. All v2.0 deviations, with the reason and what had been seen when each was decided, are in `docs/design_decisions.md` and `docs/analysis_log.md`.
